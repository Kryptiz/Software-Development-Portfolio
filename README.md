## Interpreting User Needs
**How do I interpret user needs and implement them into a program? How does creating "user stories" help with this?**

For me it starts with figuring out who is going to use the program and what they're actually trying to get done. People don't always say exactly what they need, so I try to ask questions and look at the problem from their side before I start building anything. If I can, I check in along the way so I'm not three weeks into something that misses the point.

User stories help because they keep me honest about that. Writing "As a user, I want this so that I can do that" makes me say who the feature is for and why it matters. If I can't finish that sentence, I probably don't need the feature. They also break a big idea into small pieces that are easier to build and test.

## Approaching Program Development
**How do I approach developing programs? What Agile processes do I hope to incorporate into my future development work?**

I try not to jump straight into code. I like to understand what I'm building first, sketch out a plan, and then build it in small chunks and test as I go. When I built my Java Swing slideshow project, the parts that went best were the ones I built and tested a little at a time instead of all at once.

As for Agile, there are a few things I want to carry into my future work. Short sprints appeal to me because you get feedback early instead of at the very end. I also like the idea of using velocity to estimate, since planning based on what a team has really finished is more realistic than guessing. And I think retrospectives are underrated. Taking a few minutes to talk about what worked and what didn't is an easy way to keep getting better.

## Being a Good Team Member
**What does it mean to be a good team member in software development?**

To me, it mostly comes down to being reliable and being honest. Do what you said you'd do, and if you're stuck or running behind, say so early so the team can adjust. Nobody expects perfection, but people do need to be able to count on you.

It also means communicating well and being easy to work with. That means giving feedback kindly, taking it without getting defensive, and writing code and notes that other people can actually understand. When I worked on a group project during my internship, I saw how much smoother things went when everyone shared what they were doing and helped each other out. A team is only as strong as the trust between its members.
